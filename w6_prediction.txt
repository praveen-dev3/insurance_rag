WEEK 6 PREDICTION — written 2026-08-26, BEFORE judge_v2 was authored and
before it was run. agreement_before is already known (80.0%, 20/25); what
follows is a claim about what the iteration will do to it.

THE PREDICTION, in one sentence:

    Adding w6-17 and w6-12 as few-shot examples — one for each direction
    the judge is wrong in — will fix all three too-strict cases (w6-09,
    w6-10, w6-17) and both too-lenient cases (w6-01, w6-12), taking
    agreement from 80% to 100% (25/25).


WHY I EXPECT THAT

judge_v1's five disagreements are not five problems. They are two, and
each one is the same mistake repeated:

  TOO STRICT (w6-09, w6-10, w6-17) — all three ruled UNFAITHFUL because a
  citation pointed at the wrong clause or the wrong retrieved chunk. The
  judge's own reasons say so almost verbatim:
      w6-09 "incorrectly attributes the presumption ... to Clause 4.1"
      w6-10 "cites ... Clause 5.3, but the wording places this in 4.3"
      w6-17 "cites the chunk_id for POLICY WORDING 2 ... found in 1"
  In all three the PROPOSITION was supported by wording that had been
  retrieved; only the pointer was wrong. judge_v1 already says "do not
  judge whether it states the claim number, the date of loss or the
  deductible", but it never says that a misaimed citation is somebody
  else's job — and it is: rag/assertions.py checks it deterministically.

  TOO LENIENT (w6-01, w6-12) — both ruled FAITHFUL on a summary that made
  a blanket negative claim over an exclusion table it had only partly
  been shown. w6-12 is the expensive one: it says the table "does not
  cover general water damage from a pipe split" having seen five of ten
  rows, when the sixth unseen row is E-72 and the dwelling had been
  vacant twenty weeks. The judge's reason — "the analysis of the
  exclusion table is directly supported" — shows it checked the rows in
  front of it and never asked whether those were all the rows.

Two rules, two examples, five cases. Hence 100%.


WHAT WOULD FALSIFY IT

  - If the too-strict three do not all flip, the citation instruction is
    not being read as scoping and needs to be a rule rather than a note.
  - If w6-01 does not flip while w6-12 does, the judge is keying on the
    facts of the few-shot example rather than the principle in it, and
    the example is doing memorisation instead of instruction.
  - If agreement goes UP but the lenient cell does not go to zero, the
    gain is on the cheap direction and the judge is still shipping the
    payout, which would make an improved headline number worse than
    useless.


WHERE I EXPECT TO BE WRONG

  I expect the too-strict three to flip cleanly; that instruction is
  narrow and the failure is mechanical. I am much less confident about
  w6-01. It is the borderline case in my own label file — I marked it
  unfaithful only to stay consistent with w6-12 under rule (d), and its
  blanket claim is about exclusion rows that genuinely do not apply,
  whereas w6-12's hides a row that decides the claim. A judge given the
  w6-12 example may well learn "unfaithful when the unseen row matters"
  rather than "unfaithful when you cannot know whether it matters", and
  that is a distinction it cannot make without the corpus in front of it.
  If exactly one of the two lenient cases flips, it will be w6-12, and
  the honest reading will be that the judge got the right answer for a
  reason I did not teach it.

  I also expect some risk of collateral damage: an instruction telling
  the judge to disregard misaimed citations could make it disregard a
  citation to a form that was never retrieved at all, which IS an
  unfaithfulness under rule (c). None of the 25 cases tests that, so the
  eval cannot detect it, and I am recording the gap rather than claiming
  the set covers it.
